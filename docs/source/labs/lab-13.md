                                       Laboratory work No. 13
  Methods for Studying the Spatial Structure of Organic Molecules
Topic: Configuration and conformation. Physical, spectral and chemical methods for determining spatial
                      structure. cis–trans isomerism of maleic and fumaric acids

1. Relevance of the Topic
The biological activity of a drug depends not only on its composition and constitution but also on its
three-dimensional structure: the spatial arrangement of functional groups determines how the molecule
fits the binding site of a receptor or enzyme. Geometric isomers can differ in activity as strongly as
enantiomers — for example, (Z)-tamoxifen is the active antiestrogen, while its (E)-isomer has weak
estrogenic activity.

Determining spatial structure is therefore a routine task in drug research and quality control. It is solved
by a combination of methods: X-ray crystallography, NMR and optical spectroscopy, polarimetry, dipole
moment measurements and chemical correlations. This work gives an overview of these methods and
shows, with maleic and fumaric acids as an example, how geometric isomers differ in physical and
chemical properties.


2. Learning Objectives
By the end of the session, the student must know:

  •​ The concepts of configuration and conformation; types of stereoisomerism (enantiomers,
     diastereomers including cis–trans isomers, conformers); R/S and E/Z nomenclature.
  •​ The conformations of ethane, butane and cyclohexane; axial and equatorial positions; relative
     energies of conformers.
  •​ The physical basis of the main methods for studying spatial structure and the information each
     method provides.
  •​ How cis–trans isomers differ in physical properties (melting point, solubility, dipole moment) and
     chemical behaviour.
By the end of the session, the student must be able to:

  •​ Build molecular models of conformers and stereoisomers and represent them with Newman,
     sawhorse and Fischer projections.
  •​ Carry out the cis → trans isomerisation of maleic acid and identify the product.
  •​ Use NMR coupling constants and IR data to assign the configuration of a double bond or the
     conformation of a ring substituent.


3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Configuration and conformation
The configuration of a molecule is the spatial arrangement of its atoms that distinguishes stereoisomers
and cannot be changed without breaking bonds: the arrangement of substituents about a stereogenic centre
(R/S), or about a double bond or ring (cis/trans, E/Z). Conformations are different spatial forms of the




same molecule that interconvert by rotation about single bonds; at room temperature they usually
interconvert rapidly and cannot be isolated.

In ethane, the staggered conformation is about 12 kJ/mol more stable than the eclipsed one (torsional
strain). In butane, the anti conformation is about 3.8 kJ/mol more stable than the gauche conformation
(steric interaction of the methyl groups). Cyclohexane adopts a strain-free chair conformation in which
each carbon carries one axial and one equatorial bond; large substituents prefer the equatorial position
(about 95% of methylcyclohexane is equatorial at 25 °C).

cis–trans (geometric) isomers are diastereomers and differ in all physical and chemical properties. Maleic
acid ((Z)-butenedioic acid) and fumaric acid ((E)-butenedioic acid) are a classic example. Maleic acid is
much more soluble in water, melts at a lower temperature, is a stronger acid in the first step (pKa1 1.9 vs
3.0, because an intramolecular hydrogen bond stabilises its monoanion), and forms a cyclic anhydride on
heating because its two carboxyl groups are close together. Fumaric acid does not form an anhydride and
is thermodynamically more stable.

     HOOC–CH=CH–COOH (Z, maleic acid) → HOOC–CH=CH–COOH (E, fumaric acid) (HCl, t)
In the presence of acids (or light with a trace of bromine), the double bond of maleic acid is temporarily
converted into a single bond by protonation or reversible addition of a nucleophile. Free rotation then
becomes possible, and the molecule returns to the more stable trans form.

6. Laboratory Experiments
Experiment 1 — Molecular Models: Conformations and Configurations
Principle: ball-and-stick models help visualise conformations and configurations and establish the
relationship between stereoisomers (identical, enantiomers, diastereomers).
Procedure:
  1.​ Build a model of ethane and turn it into the staggered and eclipsed conformations; draw the
      Newman projections.
  2.​ Build a model of butane and obtain the anti, gauche and eclipsed conformations by rotation about
      the C2–C3 bond; draw their Newman projections.
  3.​ Build cyclohexane in the chair and boat forms and identify the axial and equatorial bonds. Place a
      methyl group in the axial and in the equatorial position and compare the 1,3-diaxial interactions.


  4.​ Build both enantiomers of butan-2-ol, show that they cannot be superimposed, and assign the R/S
      configuration.
  5.​ Build models of maleic and fumaric acids and show that they cannot be interconverted without
      breaking the π-bond.
Result: Newman and Fischer projections and conclusions for each model recorded in the protocol.

Experiment 2 — Comparison of the Physical Properties of Maleic and Fumaric Acids
Principle: cis–trans isomers are diastereomers with different physical properties. Maleic acid forms an
intramolecular hydrogen bond and packs less efficiently in the crystal, so it is much more soluble in water
and melts lower than fumaric acid, whose molecules form a strong intermolecular hydrogen-bonded
network.
Procedure:
  1.​ Place 0.2 g each of maleic acid and fumaric acid in two test tubes, add 1 mL of cold water to each
      and shake. Compare the solubility.
  2.​ Determine the melting point of maleic acid (expected 130–135 °C). Fumaric acid does not melt in
      an open capillary; it sublimes on strong heating (above about 200 °C) — record this observation
      instead.
Result: maleic acid dissolves readily in cold water (about 78 g/100 mL), fumaric acid remains almost
undissolved (about 0.6 g/100 mL); maleic acid melts at about 130–135 °C, fumaric acid sublimes without
melting.

Experiment 3 — Isomerisation of Maleic Acid into Fumaric Acid
Principle: in the presence of concentrated HCl and on heating, the cis-isomer is converted into the
thermodynamically more stable trans-isomer; the poorly soluble fumaric acid crystallises from the hot
solution.
Procedure:
  1.​ In a 25–50 mL round-bottom flask, dissolve 2.0 g of maleic acid in 4 mL of warm water.
  2.​ In the fume hood, add 4 mL of concentrated HCl and 1–2 boiling chips, attach a reflux condenser
      and heat at gentle boiling for 15–20 minutes. Crystals of fumaric acid begin to separate from the hot
      solution.
  3.​ Cool the mixture to room temperature, then in an ice bath.
  4.​ Collect the crystals by vacuum filtration, wash them with a little cold water and dry. Weigh the
      product and calculate the yield.
  5.​ Identify the product: compare its solubility in cold water with that of maleic acid (Experiment 2)
      and check that it does not melt below 200 °C.
    HOOC–CH=CH–COOH (maleic acid, Z) → HOOC–CH=CH–COOH (fumaric acid, E) (HCl, t)
Result: white crystals, poorly soluble in cold water, that do not melt in an open capillary — fumaric acid.
Theoretical yield: 2.0 g (isomerisation does not change the molar mass).

Experiment 4 — Assignment of Spatial Structure from Spectral Data (Desk Exercise)
Principle: vicinal 1H–1H coupling constants depend on the dihedral angle between the C–H bonds
(Karplus relationship), and characteristic IR bands depend on the geometry of the molecule. These data
allow the configuration of double bonds and the conformation of rings to be assigned.


Procedure:
  1.​ Use the reference values: 3J(H,H) for trans-CH=CH 12–18 Hz, for cis-CH=CH 6–12 Hz; in
      cyclohexanes 3J(ax,ax) 8–13 Hz, 3J(ax,eq) and 3J(eq,eq) 2–5 Hz; IR out-of-plane C–H bending for
      trans-CH=CH ≈ 965 cm−1, for cis-CH=CH ≈ 690 cm−1.
  2.​ The vinyl protons of two isomeric cinnamic acids (C6H5–CH=CH–COOH) show coupling
      constants of 16.0 Hz and 12.7 Hz. Assign the E and Z configurations and justify your answer.
  3.​ Solve the additional spectral problems provided by the teacher and record your reasoning in the
      protocol.
Result: configurations and conformations assigned with justification recorded in the protocol.


7. Safety Precautions
  •​ Concentrated hydrochloric acid releases corrosive HCl vapour; measure it and carry out the reflux in
     the fume hood.
  •​ Maleic acid is harmful if swallowed, irritates the skin, eyes and respiratory tract and may cause skin
     sensitisation; avoid raising dust.
  •​ Heat the reaction flask with a water bath or heating mantle, and add boiling chips before heating.
  •​ Wear a lab coat, safety goggles, and gloves at all times; no food or drink in the laboratory.
  •​ Know the location of the eyewash station, safety shower, and fire extinguisher before starting work.
