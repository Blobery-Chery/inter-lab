           Carboxylic Acids. Preparation of m-Nitrobenzoic Acid
Topic: Carboxylic acids: structure, acidity and electrophilic substitution in aromatic acids. Preparation of
                                           m-nitrobenzoic acid

1. Relevance of the Topic
Carboxylic acids are among the most important classes of organic compounds in pharmacy. The carboxyl
group is present in non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (acetylsalicylic acid, ibuprofen, diclofenac), in
amino acids, bile acids and many metabolites. Aromatic nitro acids are key intermediates in drug
synthesis: nitrobenzoic acids are reduced to aminobenzoic acids, the building blocks of local anaesthetics
and other drug substances.

The nitration of benzoic acid is a model reaction that shows how a substituent already present on the ring
controls both the rate and the orientation of electrophilic aromatic substitution. The carboxyl group
deactivates the ring and directs the incoming nitro group to the meta position. The same principle is used
to plan the synthesis of substituted aromatic drug substances.


2. Learning Objectives
By the end of the session, the student must know:

  •​ The electronic structure of the carboxyl group and the reasons for the acidity of carboxylic acids
     (resonance stabilisation of the carboxylate anion).
  •​ The influence of substituents on acidity: electron-withdrawing groups increase it and
     electron-donating groups decrease it (pKa: benzoic acid 4.20, m-nitrobenzoic acid 3.45,
     o-nitrobenzoic acid 2.17).
  •​ The mechanism of electrophilic aromatic nitration: generation of the nitronium ion, formation of the
     σ-complex, loss of a proton.
  •​ Why the carboxyl group is a deactivating meta-director.
By the end of the session, the student must be able to:

  •​ Carry out nitration with a nitrating mixture under strict temperature control.
  •​ Isolate the product by pouring onto ice, vacuum filtration and recrystallisation.
  •​ Confirm the acidic properties and the identity of the product by qualitative tests and melting point.
  •​ Calculate the theoretical and percent yield.


3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Structure and acidity of carboxylic acids
In the carboxyl group –COOH, the carbonyl and hydroxyl groups are conjugated: the lone pair of the
hydroxyl oxygen interacts with the π-bond of C=O (p,π-conjugation). This makes the O–H bond more
polar than in alcohols and, above all, stabilises the carboxylate anion formed on ionisation. Its negative
charge is delocalised equally over both oxygen atoms, and both C–O bonds become equal in length (about
0.127 nm).




As a result, carboxylic acids (pKa ≈ 4–5) are much stronger acids than alcohols (pKa ≈ 16) and phenols
(pKa ≈ 10), but weaker than mineral acids. Carboxylic acids react with bases, active metals and — unlike
phenols and alcohols — with sodium hydrogen carbonate, releasing CO2. This is a simple test that
distinguishes carboxylic acids from phenols.

Substituents change acidity through inductive and resonance effects. Electron-withdrawing groups
(–NO2, –Cl, –CN) stabilise the carboxylate anion and increase acidity; electron-donating groups (–CH3,
–OCH3, –NH2) have the opposite effect. The ortho-nitro isomer is the strongest acid because the effect
acts at the shortest distance (the ortho effect).

3.2 Electrophilic substitution in benzoic acid
The carboxyl group withdraws electron density from the ring by both inductive (−I) and resonance (−M)
effects. Therefore benzoic acid undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution (SEAr) more slowly than
benzene (deactivation), and substitution occurs mainly at the meta position. The electrophile in nitration is
the nitronium ion NO2+, generated from nitric acid by concentrated sulfuric acid:

    HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4−
The nitronium ion attacks the π-system of the ring to form a positively charged σ-complex (arenium ion),
which then loses a proton to restore aromaticity. For attack at the ortho and para positions, one resonance
structure of the σ-complex places the positive charge on the carbon bearing the electron-withdrawing
–COOH group, which is strongly destabilising. Attack at the meta position avoids this, so the meta
product predominates: nitration of benzoic acid gives about 80% meta, 19% ortho and 1–2% para isomer.

    C6H5COOH + HNO3 → m-O2N–C6H4–COOH + H2O (H2SO4)
Because the ring is deactivated, a second nitration is much slower. Keeping the temperature low
suppresses both dinitration and oxidative side reactions.

6. Laboratory Experiments
Experiment 1 — Nitration of Benzoic Acid (Synthesis of m-Nitrobenzoic Acid)
Principle: electrophilic aromatic substitution by the nitronium ion; the deactivating –COOH group directs
the nitro group mainly to the meta position.
Procedure:
  1.​ In the fume hood, place 2.0 g (16.4 mmol) of benzoic acid in a 50 mL conical flask and add 5 mL of
      concentrated H2SO4. Stir with a glass rod and cool the flask in an ice–salt bath to 0–5 °C.
  2.​ In a separate test tube, prepare the nitrating mixture: slowly add 1.5 mL of concentrated HNO3 to
      1.5 mL of concentrated H2SO4 with cooling. Cool the mixture in the ice bath.
  3.​ Add the cold nitrating mixture dropwise to the benzoic acid solution with constant stirring, watching
      the thermometer: the temperature must not rise above 5–10 °C. The addition takes 10–15 minutes.
  4.​ Keep the mixture in the ice bath with occasional stirring for another 10–15 minutes, then let it stand
      at room temperature for 10 minutes.
  5.​ Pour the reaction mixture slowly, with stirring, onto 30–40 g of crushed ice in a beaker. The crude
      product separates as a white to pale-yellow precipitate.
  6.​ Collect the solid by vacuum filtration and wash it on the filter with several small portions of ice-cold
      water until the washings are only weakly acidic.
  7.​ Recrystallise the crude product from the minimum volume of boiling water, cool in ice, filter and
      dry the crystals in air. Weigh the product and calculate the yield.
     C6H5COOH + HNO3 → m-O2N–C6H4–COOH + H2O (H2SO4, 0–10 °C)
Result: white or pale-yellow crystals of m-nitrobenzoic acid (M = 167.12 g/mol), lit. m.p. 140–141 °C.
Theoretical yield from 2.0 g of benzoic acid: 2.74 g.
Note: the ortho isomer formed as a by-product is more soluble in water than the meta isomer and remains
mostly in the mother liquor after recrystallisation.




Experiment 2 — Acidic Properties of the Product
Principle: the carboxyl group is acidic enough to decompose hydrogen carbonate (release of CO2) and to
form water-soluble salts; the free acid is regenerated by a stronger acid. Aromatic carboxylate anions
form a coloured basic iron(III) salt in neutral solution.
Procedure:
  1.​ Reaction with NaHCO3: to a few crystals of m-nitrobenzoic acid (and, in a parallel tube, of benzoic
      acid) add 1–2 mL of NaHCO3 solution and observe.
  2.​ Salt formation and regeneration: dissolve about 0.1 g of the product in 1–2 mL of 10% NaOH, then
      add 10% HCl dropwise until the solution is acidic.
  3.​ Reaction with FeCl3: dissolve a small amount of the acid in a few drops of NaOH, neutralise
      carefully with HCl to a neutral reaction (check with indicator paper — excess acid or base spoils the
      test), and add 2–3 drops of FeCl3 solution.
    m-O2N–C6H4–COOH + NaHCO3 → m-O2N–C6H4–COONa + CO2↑ + H2O
    m-O2N–C6H4–COONa + HCl → m-O2N–C6H4–COOH↓ + NaCl
Result: effervescence (CO2) with NaHCO3; the acid dissolves in NaOH and precipitates again on
acidification; with FeCl3 a pale brown (buff) precipitate of the basic iron(III) salt forms.
Conclusion: the product is a carboxylic acid; a phenol would dissolve in NaOH but would not release
CO2 from NaHCO3.

Experiment 3 — Melting Point Determination
Principle: a pure compound melts over a narrow range close to the literature value; impurities (the ortho
isomer, unreacted benzoic acid) lower and broaden the melting range.
Procedure:
  1.​ Dry a small sample of the recrystallised product, pack it 2–3 mm high into a capillary tube and
      determine the melting point, heating slowly (1–2 °C/min) near the expected value.
  2.​ If time allows, determine the melting point of the starting benzoic acid (lit. 122 °C) for comparison.
Result: a sharp melting point at 140–141 °C confirms pure m-nitrobenzoic acid; a range starting well
below 140 °C indicates contamination and the need for a second recrystallisation.

  •​ Pour the acid reaction mixture onto ice — never add water to the concentrated acid mixture.
  •​ Nitro compounds are toxic and can be absorbed through the skin; avoid contact with the product and

  •​ Wear a lab coat, safety goggles, and gloves at all times; no food or drink in the laboratory.
  •​ Know the location of the eyewash station, safety shower, and fire extinguisher before starting work.
  •​ Collect acidic filtrates and washings in the designated acid waste container.

                                        Laboratory work No. 8
      Reactions of Carboxylic Acids and Their Derivatives with
   Nucleophilic Reagents. Synthesis of n-Butyl Acetate and Isobutyl
                               Acetate
 Topic: Nucleophilic acyl substitution. Esterification (Fischer reaction). Synthesis of n-butyl acetate and
                                             isobutyl acetate

1. Relevance of the Topic
Nucleophilic substitution at the acyl carbon is the central reaction type of carboxylic acids and their
functional derivatives — acyl halides, anhydrides, esters and amides. It underlies both the synthesis and
the breakdown of many drug substances: acetylsalicylic acid and paracetamol are made by acylation,
local anaesthetics such as procaine and benzocaine are esters, and the hydrolysis of ester and amide bonds
determines the stability and shelf life of these drugs as well as their metabolism in the body (esterases,
amidases).

Esters of lower acids and alcohols are used as solvents, extractants and flavouring agents in
pharmaceutical technology. The acid-catalysed synthesis of butyl acetates shows how an equilibrium
reaction can be driven towards the product, and trains basic preparative techniques: reflux, washing in a
separating funnel, drying and distillation.


2. Learning Objectives
By the end of the session, the student must know:

  •​ The mechanism of nucleophilic acyl substitution (addition–elimination through a tetrahedral
     intermediate).
  •​ The relative reactivity of carboxylic acid derivatives (acyl halides > anhydrides > esters ≈ acids >
     amides) and its explanation.
  •​ The mechanism of acid-catalysed esterification (Fischer reaction) and the ways of shifting its
     equilibrium.
  •​ Reactions of esters with nucleophiles: acid and alkaline hydrolysis (saponification), aminolysis,
     reaction with hydroxylamine.
By the end of the session, the student must be able to:

  •​ Carry out an esterification under reflux and work up the reaction mixture (washing, neutralisation,
     drying).
  •​ Purify a liquid product by distillation and identify it by boiling point (and refractive index, if a
     refractometer is available).
  •​ Detect the ester group by the hydroxamic acid (iron(III) hydroxamate) test and by saponification.
  •​ Calculate the theoretical and percent yield using the limiting reagent.





3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Nucleophilic acyl substitution
Carboxylic acids and their derivatives R–CO–X (X = Cl, OCOR, OR′, NH2, OH) react with nucleophiles
by a two-step addition–elimination mechanism. The nucleophile attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon
and forms a tetrahedral intermediate; the intermediate then collapses, the C=O bond is restored and the
group X leaves. The overall result is the replacement of X by the nucleophile, while the acyl group
R–CO– is preserved.

The reactivity of derivatives decreases in the series: acyl halides > anhydrides > esters ≈ carboxylic acids
> amides > carboxylate anions. Two factors determine it: the ability of X to leave (Cl− is the best leaving
group, NH2− the poorest), and the extent to which the lone pair of X donates electron density to the
carbonyl carbon by resonance, reducing its electrophilicity (strong in amides, negligible in acyl
chlorides). A more reactive derivative can always be converted into a less reactive one, but not directly
the other way round.

Typical reactions with nucleophiles: hydrolysis (H2O or OH−) gives acids or carboxylates; alcoholysis
(R′OH) gives esters; aminolysis (NH3, amines) gives amides; hydroxylamine gives hydroxamic acids;
SOCl2 or PCl5 convert the OH group of an acid into Cl, giving the most reactive acyl chlorides.

3.2 Esterification (Fischer reaction)
Carboxylic acids react with alcohols only in the presence of a strong acid catalyst (concentrated H2SO4,
p-toluenesulfonic acid). The catalyst protonates the carbonyl oxygen and makes the carbonyl carbon
electrophilic enough to be attacked by the weakly nucleophilic alcohol. Proton transfer, loss of water and
deprotonation follow. Isotope labelling (18O in the alcohol) shows that the ester oxygen comes from the
alcohol: the C–OH bond of the acid is broken, not the O–H bond.

    CH3COOH + CH3CH2CH2CH2OH ⇌ CH3COOCH2CH2CH2CH3 + H2O (H2SO4)
    CH3COOH + (CH3)2CHCH2OH ⇌ CH3COOCH2CH(CH3)2 + H2O (H2SO4)
Esterification is reversible (equilibrium constant K ≈ 4 for primary alcohols), so with equimolar amounts
only about two thirds of the reactants are converted. The yield is increased by using an excess of the
cheaper reagent (here acetic acid) or by removing water (azeotropic distillation with a Dean–Stark trap,
dehydrating agents). Acid hydrolysis of esters is the reverse reaction and is also catalysed by acids;
alkaline hydrolysis (saponification) is irreversible because the carboxylate anion formed is no longer
attacked by the alcohol.

6. Laboratory Experiments
Experiment 1 — Synthesis of n-Butyl Acetate
Principle: acid-catalysed esterification of acetic acid with n-butanol; a twofold excess of acetic acid shifts
the equilibrium towards the ester.
Procedure:
  1.​ Place 9.2 mL (7.4 g, 0.10 mol) of n-butanol and 11.5 mL (12.0 g, 0.20 mol) of glacial acetic acid in
      a 50 mL round-bottom flask. Carefully add 0.5 mL of concentrated H2SO4 with swirling, then 2–3
      boiling chips.
  2.​ Attach a reflux condenser and heat the mixture at gentle boiling for 45–60 minutes.
  3.​ Cool the flask to room temperature and pour the mixture into a separating funnel containing 20 mL
      of cold water. Shake, let the layers separate and discard the lower aqueous layer (the ester, d ≈ 0.88
      g/mL, is the upper layer).
  4.​ Wash the organic layer with 10–15 mL portions of 5% NaHCO3 until no more CO2 is evolved (vent
      the funnel frequently!), then with 10 mL of saturated NaCl solution.



  5.​ Transfer the ester to a dry conical flask and dry it over 1–2 g of anhydrous Na2SO4 for 10–15
      minutes, swirling occasionally, until the liquid is clear.
  6.​ Decant the dried liquid into a dry distillation flask, add a boiling chip and distil, collecting the
      fraction boiling at 122–127 °C in a pre-weighed receiver. Weigh the product and calculate the yield.
    CH3COOH + C4H9OH ⇌ CH3COOC4H9 + H2O (H2SO4, t)
Result: a colourless liquid with a pleasant fruity odour; b.p. 126 °C, d20 = 0.882 g/mL, nD20 = 1.3941.
Theoretical yield (limiting reagent n-butanol): 11.6 g.

Experiment 2 — Synthesis of Isobutyl Acetate
Principle: the same esterification with the branched isomeric alcohol. The product is a structural isomer
of n-butyl acetate with a lower boiling point because its branched molecules are more compact and have
weaker intermolecular (dispersion) forces.
Procedure:
  1.​ Carry out the synthesis exactly as in Experiment 1, using 9.3 mL (7.4 g, 0.10 mol) of isobutanol
      instead of n-butanol.
  2.​ In the distillation step, collect the fraction boiling at 114–119 °C.
    CH3COOH + (CH3)2CHCH2OH ⇌ CH3COOCH2CH(CH3)2 + H2O (H2SO4, t)
Result: a colourless liquid with a fruity odour; b.p. 117–118 °C, d20 = 0.871 g/mL, nD20 = 1.3902.
Theoretical yield: 11.6 g.
Note: the work is usually divided so that half of the group prepares n-butyl acetate and half isobutyl
acetate; the results are compared in the summary table.

Experiment 3 — Hydroxamic Acid Test for the Ester Group
Principle: esters react with hydroxylamine in alkaline medium (nucleophilic acyl substitution) to form
hydroxamic acids, which give intensely coloured red-violet complexes with Fe3+ ions.
Procedure:
  1.​ To 2–3 drops of the ester in a test tube add 1 mL of hydroxylamine hydrochloride solution and 0.5
      mL of 10% NaOH.
  2.​ Heat the mixture to boiling for 1–2 minutes and cool.
  3.​ Acidify with 1 M HCl (check with indicator paper) and add 1–3 drops of FeCl3 solution.
  4.​ Run a blank test with n-butanol for comparison.
    CH3COOC4H9 + NH2OH → CH3CO–NHOH + C4H9OH
    3 CH3CO–NHOH + Fe3+ → Fe(CH3CO–NHO)3 + 3H+
Result: a red-violet (burgundy) colour with the ester; the alcohol gives no colour other than the yellow of
FeCl3.

Experiment 4 — Alkaline Hydrolysis (Saponification) of the Ester
Principle: the hydroxide ion is a strong nucleophile; it converts the ester irreversibly into the carboxylate
salt and the alcohol.
Procedure:
  1.​ Place 0.5 mL of the ester and 5 mL of 2 M NaOH in a test tube; note the two layers and the fruity
      odour.



  2.​ Heat the tube in a boiling water bath for 10–15 minutes, shaking frequently.
  3.​ Observe the gradual disappearance of the ester layer and the change of odour.
    CH3COOC4H9 + NaOH → CH3COONa + C4H9OH
Result: the upper ester layer gradually disappears and the fruity odour is replaced by the odour of
butanol; the solution becomes homogeneous.

                                       Laboratory work No. 9
           Diazo and Azo Compounds. Synthesis of Iodobenzene
  Topic: Diazotization of primary aromatic amines. Reactions of arenediazonium salts with and without
                               loss of nitrogen. Synthesis of iodobenzene

1. Relevance of the Topic
Arenediazonium salts are among the most versatile intermediates in aromatic chemistry. The diazo group
can be replaced by almost any substituent (–OH, –F, –Cl, –Br, –I, –CN, –H), which gives substitution
patterns that cannot be obtained by direct electrophilic substitution. Aryl iodides such as iodobenzene are
important starting materials for modern cross-coupling reactions used in drug synthesis.

Diazotization is also a key analytical reaction in pharmacy. The pharmacopoeial identification test for a
primary aromatic amino group (sulfonamides, procaine, benzocaine) is based on diazotization followed
by azo coupling with 2-naphthol, and the quantitative nitritometric (sodium nitrite) titration is based on
the same reaction.

3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Diazotization
Primary aromatic amines react with nitrous acid (generated in situ from sodium nitrite and hydrochloric
acid) at 0–5 °C to form arenediazonium salts:

    C6H5NH2 + NaNO2 + 2HCl → C6H5N2+ Cl− + NaCl + 2H2O
The electrophile is the nitrosonium ion NO+ (or its carriers N2O3, NOCl), formed from nitrous acid in
acidic medium. It attacks the nitrogen of the free amine to form an N-nitrosamine, which isomerises to a



diazohydroxide; protonation and loss of water give the diazonium ion. An excess of acid (2.5–3
equivalents) is needed to generate HNO2, to keep the amine in solution as its salt, and to prevent coupling
of the diazonium salt with unreacted amine.

Arenediazonium ions are stabilised by conjugation of the –N≡N+ group with the aromatic ring, which is
why, unlike alkyldiazonium ions, they can exist in cold aqueous solution. On warming they decompose
with evolution of nitrogen, and in the dry state many diazonium salts explode on impact or heating.
Diazonium salts are therefore always prepared at 0–5 °C and used immediately in solution.

3.2 Reactions of arenediazonium salts
Reactions with loss of nitrogen. N2 is an excellent leaving group, so the diazo group is easily replaced:
by OH on warming in water (phenols); by Cl, Br or CN with copper(I) salts (Sandmeyer reaction); by F
on heating the tetrafluoroborate (Schiemann reaction); by H with hypophosphorous acid (deamination);
and by I with potassium iodide. Iodide needs no copper catalyst: it is easily oxidised and transfers an
electron to the diazonium ion, generating an aryl radical that combines with iodine.

    C6H5N2+ Cl− + KI → C6H5I + N2↑ + KCl
    C6H5N2+ Cl− + H2O → C6H5OH + N2↑ + HCl (t)
Reactions without loss of nitrogen. The diazonium ion is a weak electrophile and couples with strongly
activated aromatic compounds — phenols (in weakly alkaline medium) and aromatic amines (in weakly
acidic medium) — to form coloured azo compounds Ar–N=N–Ar′ (azo dyes).

    C6H5N2+ Cl− + 2-naphthol + NaOH → 1-phenylazo-2-naphthol↓ + NaCl + H2O

6. Laboratory Experiments
Experiment 1 — Diazotization of Aniline (Benzenediazonium Chloride Solution)
Principle: reaction of aniline hydrochloride with nitrous acid, generated from NaNO2 and HCl, at 0–5
°C; the end point is detected by a slight excess of HNO2, which oxidises iodide to iodine (blue colour
with starch).
Procedure:
  1.​ In the fume hood, place 6.5 mL of concentrated HCl and 6.5 mL of water in a 100 mL conical flask
      and add 2.3 mL (2.33 g, 25 mmol) of aniline with stirring. Aniline hydrochloride is formed (the
      mixture warms up).
  2.​ Cool the solution in an ice–salt bath to 0–5 °C (some aniline hydrochloride may crystallise; this
      does not interfere).
  3.​ Dissolve 1.8 g (26 mmol) of NaNO2 in 5 mL of water and cool the solution.
  4.​ Add the nitrite solution dropwise with constant stirring, keeping the temperature below 5 °C (add
      small pieces of ice directly to the mixture if necessary).
  5.​ Towards the end of the addition, test the mixture with starch–iodide paper 1–2 minutes after each
      portion. Diazotization is complete when a drop of the mixture turns the paper blue immediately.
  6.​ Keep the clear diazonium solution in the ice bath and use it at once: withdraw two 1–2 mL portions
      for Experiments 3 and 4 and use the rest for Experiment 2.
       C6H5NH2 + HCl → C6H5NH3+ Cl−
       C6H5NH3+ Cl− + NaNO2 + HCl → C6H5N2+ Cl− + NaCl + 2H2O
       2HNO2 + 2KI + 2HCl → I2 + 2NO↑ + 2KCl + 2H2O (starch–iodide test)
Result: a clear, almost colourless or pale-yellow solution of benzenediazonium chloride; the
starch–iodide paper turns blue at the end point.





Experiment 2 — Synthesis of Iodobenzene
Principle: iodide ion replaces the diazo group with evolution of nitrogen; no catalyst is required.
Procedure:
  1.​ Dissolve 4.5 g (27 mmol) of KI in 7 mL of water and add this solution slowly, with stirring, to the
      cold diazonium solution. A brown colour appears and nitrogen is evolved.
  2.​ Leave the mixture at room temperature for 15–20 minutes, then heat it on a water bath (60–70 °C)
      until the evolution of nitrogen stops. A heavy dark oil (crude iodobenzene) collects at the bottom.
  3.​ Cool the mixture and make it strongly alkaline with 10% NaOH; this converts the phenol by-product
      into water-soluble sodium phenoxide.
  4.​ Isolate the iodobenzene by steam distillation. If steam distillation is not available, transfer the
      mixture to a separating funnel, run off the lower (heavy) organic layer and wash it with 5%
      Na2S2O3 solution (to remove iodine) and then with water.
  5.​ Dry the crude product over anhydrous CaCl2, then distil it using an air condenser, collecting the
      fraction boiling at 185–190 °C in a pre-weighed receiver. Weigh the product and calculate the yield.
    C6H5N2+ Cl− + KI → C6H5I + N2↑ + KCl
Result: a colourless or pale-yellow heavy liquid, b.p. 188 °C, d = 1.83 g/mL, which darkens on exposure
to light (liberation of iodine). Theoretical yield from 25 mmol of aniline: 5.1 g.
Note: store iodobenzene in a dark bottle; if it turns brown, wash it with thiosulfate solution before use.

Experiment 3 — Hydrolysis of the Diazonium Salt (Reaction with Loss of Nitrogen)
Principle: on warming, the diazonium ion reacts with water: nitrogen is released and phenol is formed.
Procedure:
  1.​ Warm 1–2 mL of the diazonium solution in a water bath at 50–60 °C. Observe the evolution of gas
      and note the odour of phenol.
  2.​ Cool the tube and add bromine water dropwise.
    C6H5N2+ Cl− + H2O → C6H5OH + N2↑ + HCl
    C6H5OH + 3Br2 → 2,4,6-Br3C6H2OH↓ + 3HBr
Result: gas bubbles (N2) and the characteristic odour of phenol; bromine water is decolourised and a
white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol forms.

Experiment 4 — Azo Coupling with 2-Naphthol (Reaction without Loss of Nitrogen)
Principle: the diazonium ion couples with the 2-naphthoxide ion at position 1, forming the orange-red
azo dye 1-phenylazo-2-naphthol.
Procedure:
  1.​ Dissolve about 0.1 g of 2-naphthol in 2 mL of 10% NaOH and cool the solution in ice.
  2.​ Add 1 mL of the cold diazonium solution with shaking.
    C6H5N2+ Cl− + C10H7OH + NaOH → C6H5–N=N–C10H6OH↓ + NaCl + H2O
Result: an orange-red precipitate of the azo dye forms immediately.
Practical significance: the same sequence — diazotization and coupling with 2-naphthol — is the
pharmacopoeial identification test for drugs containing a primary aromatic amino group.

1. Relevance of the Topic
Hydroxy and oxo acids — lactic, malic, tartaric, citric, pyruvic and acetoacetic acids — are key
metabolites of carbohydrate and fat metabolism (glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, ketone body formation).
Many of them are used in pharmacy as active substances, excipients and buffer components (citric and
tartaric acids, calcium lactate, salicylic acid). Oxalic acid forms poorly soluble calcium oxalate, the main
component of most kidney stones.

Phenols are an equally important group: phenol and its derivatives are antiseptics, and the phenolic
hydroxyl group occurs in salicylic acid, paracetamol, adrenaline, morphine, estradiol and tocopherol.
Qualitative reactions of these compounds are used in pharmacopoeial identification tests and in clinical
laboratory diagnostics.

3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Polycarboxylic, hydroxy and oxo acids
Dicarboxylic acids (oxalic HOOC–COOH, malonic, succinic) are stronger acids than monocarboxylic
acids in their first dissociation step, because one carboxyl group withdraws electron density from the
other (oxalic acid pKa1 = 1.25). Oxalic acid is easily oxidised to CO2 — it is used as a primary standard
in permanganatometry — and forms a very poorly soluble calcium salt.




Hydroxy acids contain both hydroxyl and carboxyl groups: lactic acid CH3–CH(OH)–COOH, tartaric
acid HOOC–CH(OH)–CH(OH)–COOH, citric acid HOOC–CH2–C(OH)(COOH)–CH2–COOH, and the
phenolic salicylic acid. The neighbouring OH group increases acidity (−I effect) and allows hydroxy acids
to form stable chelate complexes with metal ions: tartrate keeps Cu2+ in solution in Fehling's reagent,
citrate in Benedict's reagent. Lactic acid, which contains the CH3–CH(OH)– fragment, gives the
iodoform reaction.

Oxo acids contain a carbonyl group: pyruvic acid CH3–CO–COOH (an α-oxo acid) and acetoacetic acid
CH3–CO–CH2–COOH (a β-oxo acid). β-Oxo acids decarboxylate easily; they and their esters (ethyl
acetoacetate) exist as an equilibrium mixture of keto and enol tautomers. The enol form gives a colour
reaction with FeCl3 and decolourises bromine water:

    CH3–CO–CH2–COOC2H5 (keto form) ⇌ CH3–C(OH)=CH–COOC2H5 (enol form)

3.2 Phenols
In phenols the hydroxyl group is bonded directly to the aromatic ring. The lone pair of oxygen is
conjugated with the π-system (+M effect). This (1) makes phenols more acidic than alcohols, because the
phenoxide ion is stabilised by delocalisation of the negative charge into the ring (phenol pKa ≈ 10,
ethanol ≈ 16), and (2) strongly activates the ring towards electrophilic substitution at the ortho and para
positions.

Phenols are weaker acids than carboxylic acids and carbonic acid: they dissolve in NaOH but do not
decompose NaHCO3. This difference is used to separate and distinguish phenols from carboxylic acids.
Because of the activated ring, phenol reacts instantly with bromine water without a catalyst, giving a
white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. Phenols (and enols) form coloured complexes with Fe3+ ions;
the colour depends on the structure: violet for phenol and salicylic acid, dark violet for resorcinol, green
for pyrocatechol; hydroquinone is oxidised by Fe3+ to p-benzoquinone.

6. Laboratory Experiments
Experiment 1 — Reactions of Oxalic Acid
Principle: oxalate ions form a white precipitate of calcium oxalate, insoluble in acetic acid but soluble in
mineral acids; oxalic acid is oxidised by permanganate in acidic medium to CO2.
Procedure:
  1.​ To 1 mL of oxalic acid solution add 1 mL of CaCl2 solution. Divide the precipitate into two tubes;
      add acetic acid to one and dilute HCl to the other.
  2.​ To 1 mL of oxalic acid solution add 1 mL of 10% H2SO4 and 2–3 drops of KMnO4 solution. Warm
      the tube in a water bath (60–70 °C) and observe.
     H2C2O4 + CaCl2 → CaC2O4↓ + 2HCl
     5H2C2O4 + 2KMnO4 + 3H2SO4 → 10CO2↑ + 2MnSO4 + K2SO4 + 8H2O
Result: a white fine-crystalline precipitate of calcium oxalate that does not dissolve in acetic acid but
dissolves in HCl; the violet colour of permanganate disappears (slowly at first, then quickly —
autocatalysis by Mn2+) with evolution of gas bubbles (CO2).

Experiment 2 — Reactions of Lactic Acid
Principle: (a) Uffelmann's test: the violet iron(III) phenolate complex is destroyed by lactic acid, which
binds Fe3+ as yellow-green iron(III) lactate; (b) the CH3–CH(OH)– fragment is oxidised and iodinated in
alkaline medium to give iodoform.


Procedure:
  1.​ Uffelmann's test: add 1–2 drops of 1% FeCl3 to 2 mL of 1% phenol solution (violet colour), then
      add lactic acid solution dropwise.
  2.​ Iodoform test: to 1 mL of lactic acid solution add 1–2 mL of 10% NaOH, then iodine–KI solution
      dropwise until a faint yellow colour persists; warm gently to 40–50 °C.
    CH3–CH(OH)–COOH + I2 / NaOH → CHI3↓ + NaOOC–COONa (schematic)
Result: (a) the violet colour changes to yellow-green; (b) a yellow precipitate of iodoform with its
characteristic odour.
Note: Uffelmann's test was used historically to detect lactic acid in gastric juice.

Experiment 3 — Reactions of Tartaric Acid
Principle: (a) potassium hydrogen tartrate is poorly soluble in water and precipitates as white crystals; (b)
tartrate, a vicinal diol, forms a soluble deep-blue chelate with Cu(OH)2 in alkaline medium.
Procedure:
  1.​ To 1 mL of tartaric acid solution add 10% KOH dropwise, shaking vigorously and rubbing the tube
      wall with a glass rod. After crystals appear, continue adding KOH in excess.
  2.​ In a second tube, prepare Cu(OH)2 by mixing 1 mL of CuSO4 solution with 2 mL of 10% NaOH
      (blue precipitate). Add tartaric acid solution dropwise with shaking.
    HOOC–CH(OH)–CH(OH)–COOH + KOH → KOOC–CH(OH)–CH(OH)–COOH↓ + H2O
    KOOC–CH(OH)–CH(OH)–COOH + KOH → KOOC–CH(OH)–CH(OH)–COOK + H2O
    tartrate + Cu(OH)2 (NaOH) → soluble deep-blue copper(II) tartrate chelate
Result: (a) a white crystalline precipitate of potassium hydrogen tartrate, which dissolves in excess KOH
(formation of the neutral salt); (b) the blue precipitate of Cu(OH)2 dissolves, giving a clear deep-blue
solution — the principle of Fehling's solution.

Experiment 4 — Distinguishing Citric Acid from Tartaric Acid with Calcium Ions
Principle: calcium citrate is less soluble in hot water than in cold: it does not precipitate from a cold
solution but separates on boiling and redissolves on cooling. Calcium tartrate precipitates in the cold.
Procedure:
  1.​ To 1 mL of citric acid solution add lime water in excess (until alkaline to indicator paper, about 3–5
      mL). Note whether a precipitate forms in the cold.
  2.​ Heat the mixture to boiling and observe; then cool the tube under running water.
  3.​ Repeat the test with tartaric acid solution for comparison.
    2 C6H8O7 + 3Ca(OH)2 → Ca3(C6H5O7)2↓ + 6H2O (on boiling)
Result: citric acid — the solution stays clear in the cold, a white precipitate of calcium citrate forms on
boiling and dissolves again on cooling; tartaric acid — a white precipitate of calcium tartrate forms
already in the cold.

Experiment 5 — Keto–Enol Tautomerism of Ethyl Acetoacetate
Principle: the enol form gives a red-violet complex with Fe3+; bromine adds rapidly to the C=C bond of
the enol and the colour disappears; the equilibrium then restores the enol form and the colour returns.





Procedure:
  1.​ Shake 2–3 drops of ethyl acetoacetate with 2 mL of water and add 1–2 drops of FeCl3 solution.
  2.​ Add bromine water dropwise with shaking until the colour just disappears. Observe the tube for 1–2
      minutes.
  3.​ Repeat the addition of bromine water several times.
    CH3–CO–CH2–COOC2H5 ⇌ CH3–C(OH)=CH–COOC2H5
    CH3–C(OH)=CH–COOC2H5 + Br2 → CH3–CO–CHBr–COOC2H5 + HBr
Result: a red-violet colour with FeCl3; it disappears after addition of bromine water and reappears within
a short time; the cycle can be repeated several times — evidence of a dynamic keto–enol equilibrium.

Experiment 6 — Reaction of Phenols with Iron(III) Chloride
Principle: phenols form coloured complexes (iron(III) phenolates) with Fe3+; the colour depends on the
number and position of the OH groups.
Procedure:
  1.​ Place 1 mL of the solutions of phenol, resorcinol, hydroquinone and salicylic acid in four test tubes,
      and 1 mL of ethanol in a fifth tube as a control.
  2.​ Add 1–2 drops of 1% FeCl3 solution to each tube and compare the colours.
    6 C6H5OH + FeCl3 → H3[Fe(OC6H5)6] + 3HCl (simplified)
Result: phenol — violet; resorcinol — dark violet; salicylic acid — violet (red-violet); hydroquinone —
transient greenish colour, then yellow (oxidation to p-benzoquinone), sometimes dark-green crystals of
quinhydrone; ethanol — no change.

Experiment 7 — Bromination of Phenol
Principle: the OH group strongly activates the ring, so bromine water brominates phenol at all three
ortho/para positions without a catalyst.
Procedure: to 1 mL of phenol solution add bromine water dropwise with shaking.

    C6H5OH + 3Br2 → 2,4,6-Br3C6H2OH↓ + 3HBr
Result: bromine water is decolourised immediately and a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol
forms.

Experiment 8 — Acidic Properties of Phenol Compared with a Carboxylic Acid
Principle: phenol (pKa ≈ 10) reacts with NaOH but is too weak an acid to decompose NaHCO3
(carbonic acid pKa1 = 6.4); carboxylic acids react with both.
Procedure:
  1.​ Place 1 mL of phenol emulsion (a turbid mixture of phenol and water) in a test tube and add 10%
      NaOH dropwise until the solution becomes clear.
  2.​ Add dilute HCl to the clear solution until it is acidic and observe.
  3.​ Add 1 mL of 5% NaHCO3 to 1 mL of phenol emulsion, and in another tube to a few crystals of
      tartaric or salicylic acid. Compare.
    C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5ONa + H2O
    C6H5ONa + HCl → C6H5OH + NaCl
    C6H5OH + NaHCO3 → no reaction


Result: phenol dissolves in NaOH (sodium phenoxide) and separates again as an emulsion on
acidification; no gas evolves with NaHCO3, whereas the carboxylic acid releases CO2.

        Amino Acids. Peptides and Proteins. Qualitative Reactions
   Topic: Structure and properties of α-amino acids. The peptide bond and levels of protein structure.
                   Qualitative and precipitation reactions of amino acids and proteins

1. Relevance of the Topic
α-Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins and the precursors of many biologically active
substances — neurotransmitters, hormones and coenzymes. Amino acids themselves are used as drugs
(glycine, glutamic acid, methionine, acetylcysteine), and peptides and proteins form a rapidly growing
class of therapeutics (insulin, oxytocin, monoclonal antibodies).

Qualitative reactions of amino acids and proteins are used to identify and assay these substances in
pharmaceutical analysis, and in clinical laboratories to detect protein in biological fluids. Understanding
precipitation and denaturation is important for the formulation and storage of protein drugs and explains,
for example, why milk and egg white are given as first aid in poisoning with heavy-metal salts.


2. Learning Objectives
By the end of the session, the student must know:

  •​ The structure, classification and stereochemistry (L-series) of α-amino acids.
  •​ The amphoteric properties of amino acids: the zwitterion and the isoelectric point.
  •​ Reactions of amino acids as amines and as acids; formation of chelate complexes.
  •​ The structure of the peptide bond and the four levels of protein structure.
  •​ The chemistry of the biuret, ninhydrin, xanthoproteic and Fohl reactions and of protein precipitation
     reactions.
By the end of the session, the student must be able to:

  •​ Detect free α-amino acids and peptide bonds in a sample.
  •​ Detect aromatic and sulfur-containing amino acid residues in proteins.
  •​ Distinguish reversible precipitation (salting out) from irreversible denaturation.
  •​ Explain each observation with an equation or a structural argument.


3. Theoretical Background
3.1 α-Amino acids
α-Amino acids have the general formula R–CH(NH2)–COOH; twenty of them (the proteinogenic amino
acids) are encoded by the genetic code. All except glycine are chiral, and natural amino acids belong to
the L-series (in the CIP system mostly S; cysteine is R).

Amino acids are amphoteric. In the solid state and in neutral solution they exist as zwitterions (inner salts)
H3N+–CH(R)–COO−, which explains their high melting points and solubility in water. The pH at which
the net charge of the molecule is zero is the isoelectric point, pI (glycine pI = 5.97). As bifunctional
compounds, amino acids react both as amines (acylation, reaction with nitrous acid, Schiff base




formation) and as acids (salt formation, esterification), and form stable chelate complexes with metal
ions, such as the deep-blue copper(II) glycinate.

The reaction with ninhydrin is a general test for α-amino acids. The amino acid is oxidatively deaminated
and decarboxylated, and the released ammonia condenses with a second ninhydrin molecule to form a
blue-violet dye (Ruhemann's purple). Proline, a secondary amine, gives a yellow colour.

    R–CH(NH2)–COOH + 2 ninhydrin → Ruhemann's purple + R–CHO + CO2 + 3H2O (schematic)

3.2 Peptides and proteins
In peptides and proteins, amino acid residues are linked by peptide (amide) bonds –CO–NH–. The peptide
bond is planar and has partial double-bond character because the nitrogen lone pair is conjugated with the
C=O group; rotation about it is restricted, and it usually adopts the trans configuration.

Protein structure is described at four levels: primary (the amino acid sequence), secondary (α-helix and
β-sheet, stabilised by hydrogen bonds between peptide groups), tertiary (the overall folding of one chain,
stabilised by disulfide bridges, ionic, hydrogen and hydrophobic interactions) and quaternary (association
of several subunits, as in haemoglobin).

Qualitative reactions of proteins include (1) the general biuret reaction for peptide bonds and (2) colour
reactions for particular residues: the xanthoproteic reaction for aromatic residues (tyrosine, tryptophan),
the Fohl reaction for sulfur-containing residues (cysteine, cystine), Millon's reaction for tyrosine and the
Sakaguchi reaction for arginine (the last two are not performed in this work). Proteins are also
characterised by precipitation reactions: reversible (salting out with neutral salts) and irreversible
(denaturation by heat, heavy-metal salts, concentrated acids and organic solvents).

6. Laboratory Experiments
Experiment 1 — Formation of Copper(II) Glycinate (Chelate Complex)
Principle: α-amino acids act as bidentate ligands (nitrogen of the amino group and oxygen of the
carboxylate) and form stable, intensely coloured chelate complexes with Cu2+.
Procedure:
  1.​ Prepare a small amount of Cu(OH)2: mix 2–3 drops of 5% CuSO4 with 3–4 drops of 10% NaOH
      (blue precipitate).
  2.​ Add 1–2 mL of glycine solution and shake.
  3.​ For comparison, add 1–2 mL of water to a second portion of Cu(OH)2.
    2 H2N–CH2–COOH + Cu(OH)2 → (H2N–CH2–COO)2Cu + 2H2O
Result: the precipitate dissolves and a dark-blue solution of copper(II) glycinate forms; with water the
precipitate remains unchanged.

Experiment 2 — Ninhydrin Reaction
Principle: free α-amino groups react with ninhydrin on heating to form the blue-violet Ruhemann's
purple.
Procedure:
  1.​ Place 1 mL of glycine solution in one test tube and 1 mL of egg white solution in another.
  2.​ Add 4–5 drops of ninhydrin solution to each tube and heat in a boiling water bath for 1–2 minutes.
Result: a blue-violet colour develops with glycine and with the protein solution (free α-amino groups of
the amino acid and of the protein).

Experiment 3 — Biuret Reaction (Test for Peptide Bonds)
Principle: in strongly alkaline medium, Cu2+ forms violet complexes with the nitrogen atoms of peptide
groups. At least two peptide bonds are required, so free amino acids do not give the violet colour.


Procedure:
  1.​ Place 1 mL of egg white solution, 1 mL of gelatin solution and 1 mL of glycine solution in three test
      tubes.
  2.​ Add 1 mL of 10% NaOH to each tube, then 1–2 drops of 1% CuSO4, and shake. Avoid excess
      CuSO4 — its blue colour masks the reaction.
Result: egg white and gelatin — violet (red-violet) colour; glycine — blue colour only (negative).

Experiment 4 — Xanthoproteic Reaction (Aromatic Amino Acid Residues)
Principle: concentrated nitric acid nitrates the activated aromatic rings of tyrosine and tryptophan
residues, forming yellow nitro derivatives; in alkaline medium they are converted into orange salts.
Procedure:
  1.​ In the fume hood, add 5–6 drops of concentrated HNO3 to 1 mL of egg white solution (a white
      precipitate of denatured protein forms) and to 1 mL of gelatin solution in another tube.
  2.​ Heat carefully in a water bath for 1–2 minutes and observe the colour.
  3.​ Cool the tubes and carefully add 30% NaOH dropwise until alkaline.
    tyrosine residue + HNO3 → 3-nitrotyrosine residue (yellow) + H2O; in NaOH → orange
    phenolate
Result: egg white — the precipitate and solution turn yellow on heating and orange after addition of
alkali; gelatin — only a weak pale-yellow colour, because gelatin contains little tyrosine and no
tryptophan.
Note: the yellow staining of skin by nitric acid is a xanthoproteic reaction of skin proteins.

Experiment 5 — Fohl Reaction (Sulfur-Containing Amino Acid Residues)
Principle: on boiling with concentrated alkali, the sulfur of cysteine and cystine residues is split off as
sulfide ion, which gives a black-brown precipitate of lead(II) sulfide with lead(II) ions. Methionine does
not react because its sulfur is bound more firmly (thioether).
Procedure:
  1.​ In the fume hood, to 1 mL of egg white solution add an equal volume of 30% NaOH and 3–4 drops
      of lead(II) acetate solution.
  2.​ Heat the mixture carefully to boiling and boil for 2–3 minutes; let it stand.
  3.​ Repeat the test with gelatin solution (and cysteine solution, if available).
    R–CH2–SH + 2NaOH → R–CH2–OH + Na2S + H2O (schematic)
    Pb(CH3COO)2 + 4NaOH → Na2[Pb(OH)4] + 2CH3COONa
    Na2S + Na2[Pb(OH)4] → PbS↓ + 4NaOH
Result: egg white — brown to black precipitate (PbS); gelatin — negative or very weak (gelatin is poor
in cysteine).

Experiment 6 — Precipitation of Proteins (Salting Out and Denaturation)
Principle: neutral salts at high concentration remove the hydration shell and charge of protein molecules
and precipitate them reversibly (salting out); heat, heavy-metal ions and organic solvents destroy the
native structure (denaturation) and precipitate proteins irreversibly.





Procedure:
  1.​ Salting out: to 2 mL of egg white solution add an equal volume of saturated (NH4)2SO4 solution
      (globulins precipitate). Filter and saturate the filtrate with solid (NH4)2SO4 (albumins precipitate).
      Dilute part of the precipitate with water.
  2.​ Heat coagulation: acidify 2 mL of egg white solution with 1–2 drops of 1% acetic acid and heat to
      boiling.
  3.​ Heavy-metal salts: add 5% CuSO4 dropwise to 1 mL of egg white solution, then add excess CuSO4.
  4.​ Organic solvent: add 2 mL of ethanol to 1 mL of egg white solution.
Result: (1) a white precipitate that redissolves on dilution with water — reversible; (2) white flakes of
coagulated protein; (3) a precipitate that may partly redissolve in excess CuSO4 (adsorption of ions); (4)
turbidity and a precipitate. Precipitation in (2)–(4) is irreversible (denaturation).
Practical significance: milk and egg white are given as first aid in poisoning with heavy-metal salts,
because their proteins bind the metal ions.

  Methods of Derivatization of Enantiomers. Common Derivatizing

Topic: Chirality, enantiomers and diastereomers. Indirect separation and analysis of enantiomers by chiral
                     derivatization. Resolution of a racemate via diastereomeric salts

1. Relevance of the Topic
Most modern drug substances are chiral, and their enantiomers often differ greatly in pharmacological
activity, metabolism and toxicity: (S)-ibuprofen is the active anti-inflammatory enantiomer and
(S)-propranolol is the active β-blocker. The history of thalidomide showed how important stereochemistry
is for drug safety. Pharmacopoeias and regulatory agencies therefore require the enantiomeric purity of
chiral drugs to be determined.

Enantiomers have identical physical properties in an achiral environment and cannot be separated or
distinguished by ordinary chromatography or NMR. The classical solution is derivatization: reaction with
an enantiomerically pure reagent converts a pair of enantiomers into a pair of diastereomers, which differ
in physical properties. They can then be separated by crystallisation or achiral chromatography, or
distinguished by NMR. This work introduces the principles of this approach and the most widely used
chiral derivatizing reagents.

  •​ The most common derivatizing reagents, the functional groups they react with, and the analytical

  •​ The difference between indirect (derivatization) and direct (chiral stationary phases, chiral solvating

3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Enantiomers, diastereomers and their properties
A chiral molecule is not superimposable on its mirror image; the two mirror-image forms are
enantiomers. In an achiral environment, enantiomers have identical melting and boiling points, solubility,
NMR and IR spectra and chromatographic behaviour. They differ only in the direction in which they
rotate plane-polarised light and in their interaction with other chiral objects (receptors, enzymes, chiral
reagents and sorbents). Diastereomers — stereoisomers that are not mirror images — differ in all physical
properties.

An equimolar mixture of enantiomers (a racemate) is optically inactive. The composition of a mixture of
enantiomers is expressed as enantiomeric excess (ee), and the optical rotation of a substance is
characterised by its specific rotation:

    ee (%) = ([R] − [S]) / ([R] + [S]) × 100
    [α] = α / (l · c)   (α — observed rotation, °; l — tube length, dm; c — concentration, g/mL)
    optical purity (%) = [α]observed / [α]pure enantiomer × 100 ≈ ee

3.2 The principle of chiral derivatization
In the indirect method, a mixture of enantiomers (R)-A and (S)-A reacts with a single, enantiomerically
pure reagent (R)-B. Two diastereomers are formed, (R)-A–(R)-B and (S)-A–(R)-B. They can be separated
by crystallisation or on an ordinary (achiral) GC or HPLC column, or distinguished by NMR because
their signals have different chemical shifts. The ratio of the diastereomers equals the original ratio of the
enantiomers.

    (R)-A + (S)-A + 2 (R)-B → (R)-A–(R)-B + (S)-A–(R)-B (diastereomers)
Requirements for a chiral derivatizing agent: it must be enantiomerically pure (traces of its enantiomer
distort the result); it must react quantitatively and at the same rate with both enantiomers (no kinetic
resolution); neither the analyte nor the reagent may racemise during the reaction; and the derivatives must
be stable and easy to detect (UV-absorbing or fluorescent groups, a 19F label for NMR).
Derivatization can also be preparative. In the classical resolution of racemates (Pasteur, 1853), a racemic
acid or base is converted into diastereomeric salts with an enantiomerically pure base or acid (brucine,
quinine, 1-phenylethylamine, tartaric acid, camphor-10-sulfonic acid). The salts differ in solubility and
are separated by fractional crystallisation; the pure enantiomer is then liberated with a strong acid or base.
This approach is used in the present work.

Experiment 2 — Resolution of Racemic 1-Phenylethylamine via Diastereomeric Salts
Principle: racemic 1-phenylethylamine reacts with enantiomerically pure (R,R)-tartaric acid to form two
diastereomeric salts. In methanol, the salt of the (S)-amine is less soluble and crystallises, while the salt of
the (R)-amine stays in solution. Treatment of the crystals with alkali liberates the (S)-(−)-amine.
Procedure:
  1.​ In a 125 mL conical flask, dissolve 3.9 g (26 mmol) of L-(+)-tartaric acid in 55 mL of methanol by
      heating on a water bath almost to boiling.



  2.​ In the fume hood, slowly and carefully add 3.1 g (3.3 mL, 26 mmol) of racemic 1-phenylethylamine
      to the hot solution in small portions (the reaction is exothermic).
  3.​ Stopper the flask and leave it to crystallise undisturbed at room temperature until the next session (at
      least 24 h). The (S)-amine salt crystallises as prisms; if needles form instead, redissolve them by
      heating and allow the solution to cool slowly.
  4.​ Collect the crystals by vacuum filtration, wash them with a small amount of cold methanol, dry in
      air and weigh.
  5.​ Dissolve the salt in about 15 mL of water and add 3 mL of 50% NaOH (the solution must be
      strongly alkaline): the free amine separates as an oily layer.
  6.​ Extract the amine with two 10 mL portions of ether, combine the extracts and dry them over
      anhydrous Na2SO4.
  7.​ Decant the solution into a pre-weighed flask and evaporate the ether on a warm water bath in the
      fume hood (no open flames!). Weigh the residual amine.
    (R,S)-C6H5CH(CH3)NH2 + (R,R)-tartaric acid → (S)-amine · (R,R)-hydrogen tartrate↓ (less
    soluble) + (R)-amine salt (in solution)
    (S)-C6H5CH(CH3)NH3+ HT− + 2NaOH → (S)-C6H5CH(CH3)NH2 + Na2T + 2H2O (T =

  Methods for Studying the Spatial Structure of Organic Molecules
Topic: Configuration and conformation. Physical, spectral and chemical methods for determining spatial
                      structure. cis–trans isomerism of maleic and fumaric acids

1. Relevance of the Topic
The biological activity of a drug depends not only on its composition and constitution but also on its
three-dimensional structure: the spatial arrangement of functional groups determines how the molecule
fits the binding site of a receptor or enzyme. Geometric isomers can differ in activity as strongly as
enantiomers — for example, (Z)-tamoxifen is the active antiestrogen, while its (E)-isomer has weak
estrogenic activity.

Determining spatial structure is therefore a routine task in drug research and quality control. It is solved
by a combination of methods: X-ray crystallography, NMR and optical spectroscopy, polarimetry, dipole
moment measurements and chemical correlations. This work gives an overview of these methods and
shows, with maleic and fumaric acids as an example, how geometric isomers differ in physical and
chemical properties.

     energies of conformers.
  •​ The physical basis of the main methods for studying spatial structure and the information each

  •​ How cis–trans isomers differ in physical properties (melting point, solubility, dipole moment) and

3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Configuration and conformation
The configuration of a molecule is the spatial arrangement of its atoms that distinguishes stereoisomers
and cannot be changed without breaking bonds: the arrangement of substituents about a stereogenic centre
(R/S), or about a double bond or ring (cis/trans, E/Z). Conformations are different spatial forms of the




same molecule that interconvert by rotation about single bonds; at room temperature they usually
interconvert rapidly and cannot be isolated.

In ethane, the staggered conformation is about 12 kJ/mol more stable than the eclipsed one (torsional
strain). In butane, the anti conformation is about 3.8 kJ/mol more stable than the gauche conformation
(steric interaction of the methyl groups). Cyclohexane adopts a strain-free chair conformation in which
each carbon carries one axial and one equatorial bond; large substituents prefer the equatorial position
(about 95% of methylcyclohexane is equatorial at 25 °C).

cis–trans (geometric) isomers are diastereomers and differ in all physical and chemical properties. Maleic
acid ((Z)-butenedioic acid) and fumaric acid ((E)-butenedioic acid) are a classic example. Maleic acid is
much more soluble in water, melts at a lower temperature, is a stronger acid in the first step (pKa1 1.9 vs
3.0, because an intramolecular hydrogen bond stabilises its monoanion), and forms a cyclic anhydride on
heating because its two carboxyl groups are close together. Fumaric acid does not form an anhydride and
is thermodynamically more stable.

     HOOC–CH=CH–COOH (Z, maleic acid) → HOOC–CH=CH–COOH (E, fumaric acid) (HCl, t)
In the presence of acids (or light with a trace of bromine), the double bond of maleic acid is temporarily
converted into a single bond by protonation or reversible addition of a nucleophile. Free rotation then
becomes possible, and the molecule returns to the more stable trans form.

                Diazo Coupling. Preparation of Methyl Orange
Topic: Azo coupling as electrophilic aromatic substitution. Azo dyes and acid–base indicators. Synthesis
                                           of methyl orange

1. Relevance of the Topic
Azo compounds — substances in which the –N=N– group links two aromatic rings — make up more than
half of all synthetic dyes. In pharmacy and medicine they are used as acid–base indicators (methyl orange,
methyl red, Congo red), as colourants and histological stains, and as drug substances: the azo dye
prontosil was the first sulfonamide antibacterial drug (it is reduced in the body to sulfanilamide), and
sulfasalazine is used to treat ulcerative colitis.

Azo coupling is also used in pharmaceutical analysis. Drugs containing a primary aromatic amino group
(sulfonamides, procaine, benzocaine) are identified by diazotization followed by coupling with
2-naphthol to form an intensely coloured azo dye. The synthesis of methyl orange combines both steps —
diazotization and coupling — in one short preparation.

  •​ The mechanism of azo coupling as electrophilic aromatic substitution in which the arenediazonium
     ion is a weak electrophile.

     (para, or ortho if para is occupied).
  •​ The optimal pH for coupling with phenols (weakly alkaline) and with aromatic amines (weakly
     acidic), and the reasons for it.
  •​ The relationship between structure and colour in azo dyes (chromophore, auxochromes) and the
     mechanism of the colour change of methyl orange.
By the end of the session, the student must be able to:

  •​ Carry out the diazotization of sulfanilic acid and its coupling with N,N-dimethylaniline at the
     correct temperature and pH.
  •​ Isolate an azo dye by salting out.
  •​ Study the indicator properties of the synthesised dye.
  •​ Calculate the yield.


3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Diazonium salts and azo coupling
Arenediazonium salts Ar–N2+ X− are obtained by diazotization of primary aromatic amines with nitrous
acid (NaNO2 + HCl) at 0–5 °C. They undergo two types of reactions: with loss of nitrogen (replacement
of the diazo group by OH, Hal, CN, H) and without loss of nitrogen — azo coupling.

In azo coupling the diazonium cation acts as an electrophile. Because its positive charge is delocalised
over the aromatic ring, it is a weak electrophile and reacts only with strongly activated rings — phenols


(as phenoxide ions) and aromatic amines (N,N-dialkylanilines, naphthylamines). Substitution takes place
para to the activating group, or ortho if the para position is occupied.

The pH of the reaction medium is critical. Phenols couple in weakly alkaline solution (pH 8–10), where
they are present as the much more reactive phenoxide ions; aromatic amines couple in weakly acidic
solution (pH 4–7). In strongly acidic medium the amine is protonated and the –NHR2+ group deactivates
the ring; in strongly alkaline medium the diazonium ion is converted into an unreactive diazotate
Ar–N=N–O−.

3.2 Azo dyes and indicators. Methyl orange
The colour of azo dyes is due to the extended conjugated system Ar–N=N–Ar (the azo group is the
chromophore). Electron-donating groups (–OH, –NR2; auxochromes) at one end of the system and
electron-withdrawing groups at the other shift absorption into the visible region. Sulfonic acid groups
make the dyes water-soluble.

Methyl orange is sodium 4-[4-(dimethylamino)phenylazo]benzenesulfonate. It is prepared by
diazotization of sulfanilic acid and coupling of the diazonium salt with N,N-dimethylaniline. Sulfanilic
acid exists as a zwitterion H3N+–C6H4–SO3− and is poorly soluble in acid, so it is first dissolved as the
sodium salt in sodium carbonate solution. On acidification in the presence of nitrite, the diazonium salt
separates as a fine precipitate of the inner salt −O3S–C6H4–N2+.

    2 H2N–C6H4–SO3H + Na2CO3 → 2 H2N–C6H4–SO3Na + CO2↑ + H2O
    H2N–C6H4–SO3Na + NaNO2 + 2HCl → −O3S–C6H4–N2+ + 2NaCl + 2H2O
    −O3S–C6H4–N2+ + C6H5N(CH3)2 → HO3S–C6H4–N=N–C6H4–N(CH3)2 (red acid form)
    HO3S–C6H4–N=N–C6H4–N(CH3)2 + NaOH → NaO3S–C6H4–N=N–C6H4–N(CH3)2 + H2O
    (methyl orange)
As an indicator, methyl orange changes colour in the pH range 3.1–4.4. In the basic (yellow) form the
molecule has the azo structure; in acidic solution it is protonated at a nitrogen atom of the azo group,
giving a cation with a quinonoid structure and a different conjugated system that absorbs at a longer
wavelength (red).

    NaO3S–C6H4–N=N–C6H4–N(CH3)2 + 4[H] → NaO3S–C6H4–NH2 + H2N–C6H4–N(CH3)2
Result: the red colour gradually fades and the solution becomes colourless.
Practical significance: reductive cleavage of the azo bond is a metabolic pathway of azo drugs: prontosil
is reduced by azoreductases to the active sulfanilamide, and sulfasalazine is cleaved by gut bacteria to
5-aminosalicylic acid and sulfapyridine.

                                      Laboratory work No. 15
         Carbohydrates. Monosaccharides. Qualitative Reactions
    Topic: Structure and properties of monosaccharides. Qualitative reactions of aldoses, ketoses and
                                             disaccharides

1. Relevance of the Topic
Carbohydrates are the main source of energy for living organisms and the structural basis of nucleic acids,
glycoproteins and glycolipids. In pharmacy, glucose solutions are used for infusion and parenteral
nutrition, fructose, lactose and sucrose serve as excipients and sweeteners, and many important drugs are
glycosides (cardiac glycosides, aminoglycoside antibiotics).

Qualitative reactions of monosaccharides are used to identify drug substances and in clinical laboratory
diagnostics. The reduction of copper(II) and silver(I) compounds by glucose was the historical basis for
detecting glucose in urine in diabetes mellitus, and Seliwanoff's test distinguishes fructose from glucose.


2. Learning Objectives
By the end of the session, the student must know:

  •​ The classification of monosaccharides (aldoses and ketoses; trioses, pentoses, hexoses) and the D/L
     series.
  •​ The open-chain (Fischer) and cyclic (Haworth) forms of glucose and fructose; pyranoses and
     furanoses; α- and β-anomers; mutarotation.
  •​ The origin of the reducing properties of monosaccharides and the interconversion of aldoses and
     ketoses in alkaline medium.
  •​ The chemistry of the Molisch, Trommer, Fehling, Tollens, Barfoed and Seliwanoff tests.
By the end of the session, the student must be able to:

  •​ Detect carbohydrates in a sample and distinguish reducing from non-reducing sugars.
  •​ Distinguish an aldose from a ketose and a monosaccharide from a disaccharide.
  •​ Identify glucose, fructose, sucrose and maltose (lactose) in unlabelled solutions.


3. Theoretical Background
3.1 Structure of monosaccharides
Monosaccharides are polyhydroxy aldehydes (aldoses) or polyhydroxy ketones (ketoses). The most
important are the aldohexose D-glucose and the ketohexose D-fructose. The D/L series is determined by
the configuration of the stereogenic centre farthest from the carbonyl group (C5 in hexoses), by
comparison with D-glyceraldehyde.

In solution, monosaccharides exist mainly as cyclic hemiacetals formed by intramolecular nucleophilic
addition of a hydroxyl group to the carbonyl group: six-membered pyranoses (glucose) or five-membered
furanoses (the fructose unit in sucrose). Cyclisation creates a new stereogenic centre — the anomeric
carbon — giving α- and β-anomers. In solution the anomers interconvert through the open-chain form
(mutarotation): the specific rotation of freshly dissolved α-D-glucopyranose (+112°) or



β-D-glucopyranose (+19°) changes to the equilibrium value of +52.7° (about 36% α and 64% β; less than
0.1% open-chain form).

3.2 Chemical basis of the qualitative reactions
Because the cyclic forms are in equilibrium with the open-chain form, monosaccharides show the
properties of both polyhydric alcohols and carbonyl compounds.

  •​ Polyol properties: vicinal hydroxyl groups dissolve Cu(OH)2 in alkaline medium at room
     temperature to form a deep-blue chelate complex (first stage of Trommer's test).
  •​ Reducing properties: the free (or potentially free) carbonyl group reduces Cu(II) to red Cu2O
     (Trommer, Fehling and Benedict tests) and Ag(I) to metallic silver (Tollens' test), and the aldose is
     oxidised to an aldonic acid. Sugars with a free hemiacetal hydroxyl (glucose, fructose, maltose,
     lactose) are reducing; sucrose, in which both anomeric carbons are bound in the glycosidic bond, is
     non-reducing.
  •​ Ketoses also reduce: in alkaline medium fructose isomerises through an enediol intermediate into
     glucose and mannose (Lobry de Bruyn–van Ekenstein transformation), so it also gives positive
     Fehling and Tollens tests.
  •​ Barfoed's test: copper(II) acetate in dilute acetic acid is a weak oxidant; in this slightly acidic
     medium monosaccharides reduce it within 2–3 minutes, while reducing disaccharides react much
     more slowly.
  •​ Dehydration reactions: concentrated acids dehydrate pentoses to furfural and hexoses to
     5-hydroxymethylfurfural, which condense with phenols to coloured products. Molisch's test
     (α-naphthol, concentrated H2SO4) is general for all carbohydrates; in Seliwanoff's test (resorcinol,
     HCl) ketoses are dehydrated much faster than aldoses and give a cherry-red colour within 1–2
     minutes.
    CH2OH–(CHOH)4–CHO + 2Cu(OH)2 → CH2OH–(CHOH)4–COOH + Cu2O↓ + 2H2O
    CH2OH–(CHOH)4–CHO + 2[Ag(NH3)2]OH → CH2OH–(CHOH)4–COONH4 + 2Ag↓ + 3NH3 +
    H2O

6. Laboratory Experiments
Experiment 1 — Molisch's Test (General Test for Carbohydrates)
Principle: concentrated sulfuric acid dehydrates carbohydrates (after hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds) to
furfural derivatives, which condense with α-naphthol to form a violet product.
Procedure:
  1.​ To 1 mL of each sugar solution add 2–3 drops of Molisch reagent and mix.
  2.​ Tilt the test tube and carefully pour 1 mL of concentrated H2SO4 down the wall so that it forms a
      separate lower layer. Do not shake.
    hexose → 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (conc. H2SO4, −3H2O); + α-naphthol → violet
    condensation product
Result: a violet ring at the interface of the two layers with all the carbohydrates tested.

Experiment 2 — Trommer's Test
Principle: at room temperature, Cu(OH)2 is dissolved by the polyol structure (chelate formation — a test
for vicinal diols); on heating, reducing sugars reduce Cu(II) to Cu(I).
Procedure:
  1.​ To 1 mL of each sugar solution add 1 mL of 10% NaOH and 3–5 drops of 5% CuSO4. Shake and
      note the colour at room temperature.
  2.​ Place the tubes in a boiling water bath for 2–3 minutes and observe the colour change.
    sugar (polyol) + Cu(OH)2 → deep-blue copper(II) chelate
    CH2OH–(CHOH)4–CHO + 2Cu(OH)2 → CH2OH–(CHOH)4–COOH + Cu2O↓ + 2H2O
Result: at room temperature all sugars give a clear deep-blue solution (positive for polyols, including
sucrose); on heating, glucose, fructose and maltose give first a yellow (CuOH) and then a red (Cu2O)
precipitate; sucrose stays blue.



Experiment 3 — Fehling's (or Benedict's) Test
Principle: reducing sugars reduce the deep-blue copper(II) tartrate (or citrate) complex to red copper(I)
oxide on heating.
Procedure: mix 1 mL of Fehling's solution A and 1 mL of Fehling's solution B (or take 2 mL of
Benedict's reagent), add 1 mL of the sugar solution and heat in a boiling water bath for 2–3 minutes.

    RCHO + 2Cu2+ + 5OH− → RCOO− + Cu2O↓ + 3H2O
Result: a brick-red precipitate with glucose, fructose and maltose (lactose); sucrose — negative (the
solution remains blue).

Experiment 4 — Tollens' Test (Silver Mirror)
Principle: reducing sugars reduce the diamminesilver(I) complex to metallic silver.
Procedure: to 2 mL of freshly prepared Tollens' reagent in a clean test tube add 3–5 drops of the sugar
solution and warm in a water bath at 50–60 °C for a few minutes without shaking.

    CH2OH–(CHOH)4–CHO + 2[Ag(NH3)2]OH → CH2OH–(CHOH)4–COONH4 + 2Ag↓ + 3NH3 +
    H2O
Result: a silver mirror (or grey-black precipitate) with glucose, fructose and maltose; sucrose — negative.
Important: prepare Tollens' reagent immediately before use and never store it; acidify and discard the
residues right after the experiment.

Experiment 5 — Barfoed's Test (Monosaccharides vs Disaccharides)
Principle: in weakly acidic medium, copper(II) acetate is a weak oxidant: monosaccharides reduce it
quickly, reducing disaccharides only after prolonged heating.
Procedure: to 2 mL of Barfoed's reagent add 1 mL of the sugar solution (glucose, fructose, maltose or
lactose). Place all the tubes in a boiling water bath at the same time and note when a red precipitate
appears; stop the observation after 5 minutes.
Result: monosaccharides — a red precipitate of Cu2O within 2–3 minutes; disaccharides — no
precipitate within this time (a precipitate may appear after 10 minutes or more because of partial
hydrolysis, and must not be interpreted as positive).

Experiment 6 — Seliwanoff's Test (Ketoses vs Aldoses)
Principle: in hot hydrochloric acid, ketohexoses are rapidly dehydrated to 5-hydroxymethylfurfural,
which condenses with resorcinol to form a cherry-red product; aldohexoses react much more slowly.
Procedure: to 2 mL of Seliwanoff's reagent add 0.5 mL of the sugar solution (glucose, fructose, sucrose).
Place the tubes in a boiling water bath at the same time and observe for no more than 1–2 minutes.

    fructose → 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HCl, t, −3H2O); + resorcinol → cherry-red product
Result: fructose — cherry-red colour within about 1 minute; sucrose — red colour after a short delay
(acid hydrolysis releases fructose); glucose — no colour, or only faint pink after prolonged heating.
